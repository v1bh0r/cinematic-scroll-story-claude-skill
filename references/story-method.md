# Story method

The scene has to land in the mind and the heart. The mind wants a clear claim per screen; the heart
wants something to *happen*: loss, risk, a decision, relief. Most "animated heroes" only decorate.
This method makes the animation carry the argument.

## 1. Extract from the site

| Question | Where to look | Becomes |
|---|---|---|
| What is the one promise? | Hero, meta description, sales deck | The hero title and lede |
| Whose pain, in whose words? | Case studies, testimonials, FAQ, competitor pages | Act one's copy and event |
| What are the steps of the method? | "How it works", playbooks, docs | Act two's stations (3–6) |
| Where can it fail, and what then? | Guarantees, QA, governance, "what if" | The tension beat |
| What stays with a person? | Human-in-the-loop, support, approvals, expertise | The decision beat |
| What is the after? | Outcomes, case-study results | Act three's payoff and the CTA |
| What is the real proof? | Published numbers with links, partner tiers, logos | Below the film, never inside it |
| What must never appear? | Confidential clients, internal figures, unshipped claims | A written exclusion list |

Use the site's own vocabulary for its audience. If a VP of Engineering would not say "estate" or
"fork", neither does the page.

## 2. Three acts

1. **The world as it is.** Establish the world and its rule, then **agitate**: show the problem
   happening, and don't just state it. PAS says agitation is the most skipped step. A labelled outage,
   a queue that stalls or a light that fails does more than a paragraph.
2. **The method, walked.** One station per beat. Put **tension** in the middle:
   - one station where something fails and the method uses the failure;
   - one station where a person decides on screen, the change waits until they do, and the moment
     gets its own close-up.

   Six successes in a row is a brochure.
3. **The transformation.** The rule pays off, visibly and gradually, one element at a time. It lands
   on the outcome in the visitor's terms, then the CTA.

A good check is the PAIPS sequence: Problem, Agitate, Intrigue (hero hook), Positive future (act three),
Solution (the stations). The DR hook rule applies too: something must move in the first 3 seconds.

## 3. The rule

Pick **one** visual element and give it **one** meaning, stated in a sentence. Examples of the form:
"light means people", "water finds its level when the system is healthy", "colour returns as trust
is earned", "the path straightens as risk is removed".

Then audit every beat against it:
- Does anything obey the rule in one act and break it in another? (A real case: "the factory's lights
  go out", then "the lights come back on" confused viewers about *whose* lights. It was fixed by
  stating whose light it was each time.)
- Does the payoff *use* the rule? The ending should be the rule fulfilled.
- Does the rule explain the exceptions? ("One window stays lit: the decisions stay yours.")

## 4. Metaphor on screen, reality in words

The picture is a metaphor, and the copy and callouts name the real things.
- Callouts label metaphorical objects as the real systems, people or outcomes, each with what is
  wrong and, later, what it became: "Payments · Spring 4.3, out of support" → "Payments · on a
  supported stack".
- Never write the metaphor into the copy ("the city lights up"). Write the reality ("your systems
  come back to your people").
- Problems should look fine from a distance and fail up close, if that is true of the domain.
  Legacy risk is invisible until it isn't.

## 5. Beat sheet

Write one row per beat before any code:

| # | Shot (where, what's in frame, where the text sits) | Event on its own clock | Eyebrow · headline · ≤2 sentences | Callouts | Feeling |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Rules for the copy:
- One claim per screen, and about 25–40 words per beat.
- The headline is a statement a person would say.
- No insider jargon, no stacked adjectives, no "seamless/robust/unlock".
- Run a stop-slop pass: cut throat-clearing, triplets, em-dash chains and "not X, but Y" tics.

## 6. Director's pass (after it runs)

Ask of every beat:
- What happens here that did not happen in the last one?
- Where is the camera's motivation to move?
- Where does the eye go first?
- Is the subject clear of the text?
- Would you notice if this beat were deleted?

Then ask of the whole film:
- **The hook.** Does the first frame move? An opening push that is already in motion works.
- **The variety.** Is there a close-up and a high angle, or does every shot use the same grammar?
- **The pacing.** Is there time to *bask*? Hold on arrivals and let events play out on clocks, not
  on scroll.
- **Sound.** Is it opt-in, and does it follow the rule? The Twilight Factory had a hum while people
  worked, a crackle at the failure, a chime at the decision, and a rising note per restored element.
